# Sampling Theory and Aliasing

A camera lens forms a continuous optical image on the image plane. The image sensor converts this continuous image into a discrete digital image.

This raises an important question:

> How densely should the continuous image be sampled so that important information is preserved without introducing artifacts?

The study of this question is called **sampling theory**.

---

## 1. From a Continuous Image to a Digital Image

![Continuous and Discrete Signals](../artifacts/continuous-discrete-sampling.png)

A lens forms a continuous image on the image plane. The sensor samples this image at discrete pixel locations.

In one dimension, let $f(x)$ represent the continuous signal. Sampling means measuring the signal at regularly spaced positions:

$$
x=n x_0
$$

where:

* $x_0$ is the distance between consecutive samples
* $n$ is an integer sample index

The sampling frequency is therefore:

$$
u_s=\frac{1}{x_0}
$$

A smaller sample spacing $x_0$ gives a higher sampling frequency and more samples per unit distance.

---

## 2. What Happens When the Sampling Rate Is Too Low?

![Sampling a Sinusoid](../artifacts/sampling-sinusoid.png)

Consider a low-frequency sinusoid and a higher-frequency sinusoid sampled at the same rate.

For the low-frequency signal, the samples describe the original waveform well. Interpolation between the samples produces a reconstruction that is close to the continuous signal.

For the higher-frequency signal, the same sample spacing may be insufficient. The samples may appear to form a much slower signal or even a constant signal. Important information is lost.

This incorrect appearance of a frequency is called **aliasing**.

Aliasing can also create frequencies and patterns that were not present in the original signal.

---

## 3. Aliasing in Images

![Image Aliasing and Moire Patterns](../artifacts/image-aliasing-moire.png)

A brick wall or finely textured surface may contain details whose spatial frequency is close to or greater than the sensor's sampling limit.

If the image is sampled sufficiently densely, the texture appears correctly.

If it is undersampled, false patterns may appear, including:

* ripples
* waves
* false stripes
* moiré patterns
* incorrect textures

These artifacts are a visual manifestation of aliasing.

---

## 4. Modeling Sampling with an Impulse Train

To analyze sampling mathematically, we use an impulse train, also called a **shah function**.

Let the sampling period be $x_0$. The impulse train is:

$$
s(x)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{+\infty}\delta(x-nx_0)
$$

The sampled signal is obtained by multiplying the continuous signal by the impulse train:

$$
f_s(x)=f(x)s(x)
$$

Using the sifting property of the delta function:

$$
f_s(x)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{+\infty}f(nx_0)\delta(x-nx_0)
$$

This expression shows that the sampled signal contains the original signal values at the discrete sampling locations.

---

## 5. The Fourier Transform of the Impulse Train

![Impulse Train Sampling](../artifacts/impulse-train-sampling.png)

An important property of the shah function is that its Fourier transform is also an impulse train.

If the impulse train has spatial period $x_0$, then its frequency-domain impulses are separated by:

$$
\nu_s=\frac{1}{x_0}
$$

In simplified form:

$$
\mathcal{F}\{s(x)\}
\propto
\sum_{k=-\infty}^{+\infty}\delta(\nu-k\nu_s)
$$

Thus, a regular sampling grid in the spatial domain produces a regular repetition pattern in the frequency domain.

---

## 6. Sampling in the Frequency Domain

Because sampling is multiplication in the spatial domain, it becomes convolution in the frequency domain.

Starting with:

$$
f_s(x)=f(x)s(x)
$$

we obtain:

$$
F_s(\nu)=F(\nu)*S(\nu)
$$

Since $S(\nu)$ is an impulse train, convolution creates repeated copies of the original spectrum $F(\nu)$.

The copies are separated by the sampling frequency:

$$
\nu_s=\frac{1}{x_0}
$$

Therefore, sampling produces multiple shifted copies of the original frequency spectrum.

---

## 7. Nyquist Sampling Condition

![Nyquist Sampling Condition](../artifacts/nyquist-sampling.png)

Let $\nu_{\max}$ be the highest frequency present in the continuous signal.

The repeated spectra do not overlap if:

$$
\nu_{\max}\leq\frac{\nu_s}{2}
$$

Since $\nu_s=1/x_0$, this can also be written as:

$$
\nu_{\max}\leq\frac{1}{2x_0}
$$

The quantity:

$$
\nu_N=\frac{\nu_s}{2}
$$

is called the **Nyquist frequency**.

The Nyquist condition is therefore:

> The sampling frequency must be at least twice the highest frequency in the signal.

Equivalently:

$$
\nu_s\geq 2\nu_{\max}
$$

When this condition is satisfied, the signal is sufficiently sampled in the ideal theoretical setting.

---

## 8. Recovering the Original Signal

If the repeated spectra do not overlap, one copy of the original spectrum can be isolated using a low-pass reconstruction filter.

Define an ideal frequency-domain window:

$$
C(\nu)=
\begin{cases}
1, & |\nu|\leq\nu_N\\
0, & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
$$

Multiplying the sampled spectrum by this window extracts one copy:

$$
F(\nu)=F_s(\nu)C(\nu)
$$

The original continuous signal is then recovered by applying the inverse Fourier transform:

$$
f(x)=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\{F(\nu)\}
$$

This recovery is possible only when the spectral copies do not overlap.

---

## 9. What Happens When Spectra Overlap?

If:

$$
\nu_{\max}>\frac{\nu_s}{2}
$$

then neighboring copies of the spectrum overlap.

In the overlap region, different original frequency components are added together. There is no unique way to determine which part came from which spectral copy.

Consequently:

* the original continuous signal cannot be perfectly recovered
* new apparent frequencies are created
* the resulting artifacts are called aliasing

Once aliasing has occurred, increasing the quality of interpolation cannot restore the lost information. The information has already been combined during sampling.

---

## 10. Sampling Natural Images

![Natural Image Frequency Spectrum](../artifacts/natural-image-spectrum.png)

Natural images often have a spectrum whose magnitude decreases approximately with frequency. This behavior is sometimes described as a $1/\nu$ fall-off.

Even though high frequencies may be weaker, they can still contain important information such as:

* fine texture
* object boundaries
* repeated patterns
* small details

The camera should sample at a frequency at least twice the highest significant frequency in the continuous image. If it does not, aliasing may occur.

---

## 11. How Cameras Reduce Aliasing

![Camera Anti-Aliasing](../artifacts/camera-anti-aliasing.png)

The camera cannot control all the frequencies present in the scene. However, it can limit the frequency content that reaches the sampling grid.

Two important mechanisms help reduce aliasing.

### 11.1 Finite Pixel Area

A sensor pixel is not an ideal point sample. It has a finite area and collects the light falling over that area.

A simple model is:

1. convolve the continuous image with a box filter
2. sample the smoothed image at discrete pixel locations

If $b(x,y)$ is the pixel-area response, then:

$$
f_{\text{pixel}}(x,y)=f(x,y)*b(x,y)
$$

The box filter performs local averaging, which acts as a low-pass filter and reduces high-frequency content before sampling.

### 11.2 Optical Low-Pass Filter

A camera may also place an optical filter above the sensor. This filter attenuates frequencies above or near the Nyquist frequency before the image is sampled.

Together, the finite pixel area and the optical low-pass filter reduce the chance that high-frequency scene content will overlap in the sampled spectrum.

---

## 12. Summary

Sampling converts a continuous optical image into a discrete digital image.

The main ideas are:

* the sample spacing is $x_0$
* the sampling frequency is $\nu_s=1/x_0$
* sampling can be modeled by multiplying a signal by an impulse train
* multiplication in the spatial domain becomes convolution in the frequency domain
* sampling creates repeated copies of the original frequency spectrum
* the Nyquist frequency is $\nu_N=\nu_s/2$
* perfect recovery requires:
  $$
  \nu_s\geq2\nu_{\max}
  $$
* overlapping spectral copies cause aliasing
* aliasing creates false frequencies and visible artifacts
* finite pixel areas and optical low-pass filters help prevent aliasing

Sampling theory explains why cameras need an appropriate pixel density and why anti-aliasing is an important part of image-sensor design.
