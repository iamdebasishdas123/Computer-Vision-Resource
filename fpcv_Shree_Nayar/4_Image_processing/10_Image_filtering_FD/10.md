# Image Filtering in the Frequency Domain

Image filtering can be performed in the spatial domain or in the frequency domain. In the frequency domain, an image is represented by the spatial frequencies that make up its structure.

Because images are two-dimensional, we first extend the one-dimensional Fourier transform to two dimensions.

---

## 1. The Two-Dimensional Fourier Transform

![Two-Dimensional Fourier Transform](../artifacts/2d-fourier-transform.png)

Let $f(x,y)$ be a continuous image, where $x$ and $y$ are spatial coordinates. Let $u$ and $v$ be the corresponding spatial-frequency coordinates.

The two-dimensional Fourier transform is:

$$
F(u,v)=
\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}
\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}
 f(x,y)e^{-i2\pi(ux+vy)}\,dx\,dy
$$

The inverse transform is:

$$
f(x,y)=
\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}
\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}
F(u,v)e^{+i2\pi(ux+vy)}\,du\,dv
$$

Here:

* $x$ and $y$ describe position in the image
* $u$ is frequency along the $x$ direction
* $v$ is frequency along the $y$ direction
* $F(u,v)$ contains both magnitude and phase information

For a discrete image, the spatial coordinates are usually represented by $m,n$, and the frequency coordinates by $p,q$. The two-dimensional discrete Fourier transform is:

$$
F[p,q]=
\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}
 f[m,n]e^{-i2\pi\left(\frac{pm}{M}+\frac{qn}{N}\right)}
$$

The inverse discrete transform is:

$$
f[m,n]=
\frac{1}{MN}
\sum_{p=0}^{M-1}\sum_{q=0}^{N-1}
F[p,q]e^{+i2\pi\left(\frac{pm}{M}+\frac{qn}{N}\right)}
$$

---

## 2. Magnitude and Phase

The Fourier transform is complex and can be written as:

$$
F(u,v)=|F(u,v)|e^{i\phi(u,v)}
$$

The magnitude is:

$$
|F(u,v)|=\sqrt{\operatorname{Re}(F)^2+\operatorname{Im}(F)^2}
$$

The phase is:

$$
\phi(u,v)=\operatorname{atan2}\left(\operatorname{Im}(F),\operatorname{Re}(F)\right)
$$

For visualization, the logarithm of the magnitude is often displayed:

$$
M(u,v)=\log\left(1+|F(u,v)|\right)
$$

The logarithm compresses a wide range of magnitudes so that weak and strong frequencies can be displayed together. It is used only for visualization and does not discard the phase information.

---

## 3. Fourier Transform of a Cosine Image

![Cosine Images in the Frequency Domain](../artifacts/frequency-domain-cosine.png)

Consider an image containing a cosine that varies along the $x$ direction. A cosine with frequency $k$ produces two frequency components at $+k$ and $-k$.

If the image has a positive mean value, it also contains a zero-frequency or **DC component** at the center of the Fourier spectrum.

Therefore, the Fourier transform may show three prominent points:

* one at the center, corresponding to zero frequency
* one at $+k$
* one at $-k$

If the cosine frequency increases, the two nonzero-frequency points move farther from the center. Frequency increases with distance from the center of a centered Fourier spectrum.

If two cosine images with different frequencies are added or averaged, the spectrum contains the center component and two pairs of nonzero-frequency points.

---

## 4. Fourier Transform of Simple Objects

![Fourier Transform of Simple Objects](../artifacts/frequency-domain-objects.png)

The Fourier transform also reveals structural properties of objects.

### Directional edges

Strong edges in particular directions produce strong frequency components along related directions in the Fourier domain. Fine edges require higher frequencies than broad, slowly changing regions.

### Flat regions

A flat region contributes mainly low frequencies, including the DC component.

### Circular objects

A rotationally symmetric object produces a rotationally symmetric Fourier magnitude. A disk contains low frequencies because of its broad flat region and high frequencies because of its boundary.

The high frequencies are needed to reconstruct sharp edges in different directions.

---

## 5. Natural and Complex Images

![Fourier Transform of Natural Images](../artifacts/frequency-domain-natural-images.png)

Natural images are usually more difficult to interpret directly in the frequency domain.

For an image such as a Rubik's cube, strong lines in the Fourier magnitude may appear because the image contains edges in several dominant directions:

* vertical edges
* horizontal edges
* tilted edges

Each dominant edge direction contributes corresponding frequency structure.

For a complex natural image, such as a face or an animal, the Fourier spectrum may contain many overlapping components. It is often difficult to predict the exact magnitude and phase from visual inspection alone.

Noise is also important. Noise often contributes significant energy at high frequencies, which is why frequency-domain filtering is useful for noise reduction.

---

## 6. Low-Pass Filtering

![Low-Pass Filtering](../artifacts/frequency-domain-low-pass.png)

A low-pass filter preserves low frequencies and suppresses high frequencies.

If $F(u,v)$ is the Fourier transform of an image and $H_{LP}(u,v)$ is a low-pass filter, then:

$$
G(u,v)=F(u,v)H_{LP}(u,v)
$$

The filtered image is obtained by applying the inverse Fourier transform:

$$
 g(x,y)=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\{G(u,v)\}
$$

Low-pass filtering removes rapid changes in brightness. The result is a smoother and blurrier image.

A hard cutoff can produce ringing or blocky artifacts because the frequency response changes abruptly. A smoother transition between passed and rejected frequencies generally produces more natural results.

As the passband becomes smaller, more high-frequency information is removed and the image becomes increasingly blurred.

---

## 7. High-Pass Filtering

![High-Pass Filtering](../artifacts/frequency-domain-high-pass.png)

A high-pass filter suppresses low frequencies and preserves high frequencies.

If $H_{HP}(u,v)$ is a high-pass filter, then:

$$
G(u,v)=F(u,v)H_{HP}(u,v)
$$

High-pass filtering removes slowly varying brightness and flat regions while preserving rapid brightness changes.

As the cutoff becomes more restrictive:

* broad intensity variations disappear
* edges become more prominent
* corners and fine details dominate

This explains why high-pass filtering can be used as a basis for edge and corner detection.

---

## 8. Gaussian Smoothing in the Frequency Domain

![Gaussian Smoothing in the Frequency Domain](../artifacts/frequency-domain-gaussian.png)

Suppose an image is convolved with a Gaussian kernel in the spatial domain:

$$
 g=f*G_\sigma
$$

By the convolution theorem, the same operation can be performed in the frequency domain:

$$
G(u,v)=F(u,v)\,G_\sigma(u,v)
$$

The Fourier transform of a Gaussian is also a Gaussian. It acts as a smooth low-pass filter, gradually reducing high frequencies instead of cutting them off abruptly.

The procedure is:

1. Compute the Fourier transform of the image.
2. Compute the Fourier transform of the Gaussian kernel.
3. Multiply the two transforms.
4. Compute the inverse Fourier transform.

A narrow Gaussian kernel in the spatial domain produces a broad Gaussian response in the frequency domain and causes relatively mild smoothing.

A wider spatial Gaussian produces a narrower frequency response and causes stronger blur.

---

## 9. Why Phase Matters

![Magnitude and Phase Comparison](../artifacts/fourier-phase-magnitude.png)

The Fourier transform contains both magnitude and phase. Although frequency-domain visualizations often show only the magnitude, the phase is extremely important for preserving recognizable image structure.

If the phase is replaced by zero while the original magnitude is preserved, the reconstructed image becomes largely unrecognizable.

Conversely, if the original phase is preserved while the magnitude is replaced by an average magnitude obtained from unrelated natural images, the reconstructed image can still retain recognizable structure.

This demonstrates that the phase contains important information about the location and arrangement of image features.

---

## 10. Hybrid Images

![Hybrid Images](../artifacts/hybrid-images.png)

A hybrid image combines the low-frequency information from one image with the high-frequency information from another.

For example:

1. Low-pass filter an image of Marilyn Monroe to preserve its broad, low-frequency structure.
2. High-pass filter an image of Albert Einstein to preserve its fine details.
3. Add or average the two filtered images.

The resulting hybrid image can appear different depending on viewing distance.

At close range, the viewer sees the high-frequency details, so the image resembles Einstein. At a greater distance, the eye's own optical system acts as a low-pass filter. Fine details become less visible, and the low-frequency structure makes the image resemble Marilyn Monroe.

This example shows how spatial frequency affects visual perception.

---

## 11. Summary

Frequency-domain filtering provides another way to analyze and process images.

The main ideas are:

* images can be represented using a two-dimensional Fourier transform
* the Fourier transform contains magnitude and phase
* logarithmic magnitude displays help visualize a wide range of frequencies
* low frequencies describe broad, slowly varying image structure
* high frequencies describe edges, corners, fine texture, and noise
* low-pass filtering produces smoother images
* high-pass filtering emphasizes edges and fine details
* Gaussian smoothing can be performed by multiplying Fourier transforms
* phase is essential for preserving recognizable image structure
* hybrid images combine low-frequency information from one image with high-frequency information from another

Frequency-domain filtering is useful both for efficient computation and for understanding which image structures are affected by a filter.
