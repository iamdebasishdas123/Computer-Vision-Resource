# Deconvolution and Motion Deblurring

During image capture, an image may be unintentionally convolved with a blur kernel. A common example is motion blur caused by camera shake.

The goal of **deconvolution** is to estimate the original sharp image from the blurred image and the blur function.

Deconvolution is often easier to formulate in the frequency domain using the Fourier transform.

---

## 1. The Motion-Blur Problem

![Motion Blur](../artifacts/motion-blur.png)

Let $f(x,y)$ represent the ideal sharp image of a scene.

When the camera moves during exposure, the image is smeared. We model this smearing as convolution with a point spread function (PSF) $h(x,y)$:

$$
g(x,y)=f(x,y)*h(x,y)
$$

where:

* $f(x,y)$ is the ideal image
* $h(x,y)$ is the motion-blur point spread function
* $g(x,y)$ is the captured blurred image

The deconvolution problem is:

> Given $g$ and $h$, estimate $f$.

The point spread function may be measured or estimated from the camera motion. For example, a smartphone may contain an inertial measurement unit (IMU) with accelerometers and gyroscopes. These sensors can help estimate the camera motion during exposure and construct an approximate motion-blur kernel.

---

## 2. Inverse Filtering

![Inverse Filtering](../artifacts/inverse-filtering.png)

In practice, the recovered image may not be exactly equal to the original image. We therefore call the estimate $f'$.

The estimated image should satisfy:

$$
f' * h = g
$$

Taking the Fourier transform gives:

$$
F'(u,v)H(u,v)=G(u,v)
$$

If $H(u,v)$ is nonzero, we can formally solve for the recovered spectrum:

$$
F'(u,v)=\frac{G(u,v)}{H(u,v)}
$$

The recovered image is then:

$$
f'(x,y)=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left\{\frac{G(u,v)}{H(u,v)}\right\}
$$

This procedure is called **inverse filtering**.

The procedure is:

1. Compute the Fourier transform of the blurred image.
2. Compute the Fourier transform of the PSF.
3. Divide the image spectrum by the PSF spectrum.
4. Apply the inverse Fourier transform.

If the image contains no noise and $H$ is known accurately, inverse filtering can produce an excellent reconstruction.

---

## 3. Adding Noise to the Problem

![Noisy Motion Blur](../artifacts/noisy-motion-blur.png)

Real images also contain noise.

During exposure, camera motion creates the blur. During sensor readout, additional noise can be introduced by processes such as:

* photon-to-electron conversion
* electron-to-voltage conversion
* read noise
* quantization noise
* thermal or terminal noise

A more realistic model is:

$$
g(x,y)=f(x,y)*h(x,y)+n(x,y)
$$

where $n(x,y)$ is the added noise.

In the frequency domain:

$$
G(u,v)=F(u,v)H(u,v)+N(u,v)
$$

Applying inverse filtering gives:

$$
F'(u,v)=\frac{G(u,v)}{H(u,v)}
=
F(u,v)+\frac{N(u,v)}{H(u,v)}
$$

The second term explains the problem: if $H(u,v)$ is small, even a modest amount of noise is greatly amplified.

As a result, direct inverse filtering may produce an image overwhelmed by noise.

---

## 4. Why Inverse Filtering Becomes Unstable

![Inverse Filtering Noise Amplification](../artifacts/deconvolution-noise-amplification.png)

Motion blur behaves approximately like a low-pass filter. It reduces the magnitude of high-frequency components.

At high frequencies:

* the blur spectrum $H(u,v)$ may be close to zero
* the noise spectrum $N(u,v)$ may still have significant magnitude
* dividing by $H(u,v)$ amplifies the noise

If:

$$
H(u,v)=0
$$

then the corresponding frequency cannot be recovered at all, because division by zero is undefined. Even when $H(u,v)$ is merely very small, the recovery is unstable.

This is why inverse filtering can produce severe graininess or salt-and-pepper-like artifacts instead of a clean reconstruction.

A successful method must suppress unreliable frequencies rather than trying to recover them aggressively.

---

## 5. Wiener Deconvolution

![Wiener Deconvolution](../artifacts/wiener-deconvolution.png)

Wiener deconvolution modifies inverse filtering so that frequencies with unreliable information are attenuated.

A common form is:

$$
F'(u,v)
=
\frac{1}{H(u,v)}
\left[
\frac{|H(u,v)|^2}{|H(u,v)|^2+K(u,v)}
\right]
G(u,v)
$$

where $K(u,v)$ represents the noise-to-signal power ratio.

Equivalently, when the noise-to-signal ratio is denoted by $\operatorname{NSR}(u,v)$:

$$
F'(u,v)
=
\frac{1}{H(u,v)}
\left[
\frac{1}{1+\frac{\operatorname{NSR}(u,v)}{|H(u,v)|^2}}
\right]
G(u,v)
$$

The bracketed term is the suppression factor.

### Interpretation

If the noise is large at a frequency, then $\operatorname{NSR}$ is large. The suppression factor becomes small, so the recovery of that frequency is reduced.

If $|H(u,v)|$ is small, the factor $\operatorname{NSR}/|H(u,v)|^2$ becomes large. Again, the suppression factor becomes small, preventing strong noise amplification.

Thus, Wiener deconvolution is conservative: it recovers reliable frequencies while suppressing frequencies that are dominated by noise or have been removed by the blur.

---

## 6. Estimating the Noise-to-Signal Ratio

Ideally, Wiener deconvolution would use the noise power and signal power at every frequency. However, both are generally unknown because the original image is exactly what we are trying to recover.

A practical approach is to use a constant approximation:

$$
\operatorname{NSR}(u,v)\approx\lambda
$$

where $\lambda$ is a chosen constant.

The recovered spectrum then becomes:

$$
F'(u,v)
=
\frac{1}{H(u,v)}
\left[
\frac{1}{1+\frac{\lambda}{|H(u,v)|^2}}
\right]
G(u,v)
$$

The value of $\lambda$ controls the trade-off:

* a smaller value performs stronger deblurring but may amplify more noise
* a larger value suppresses noise more strongly but may leave more blur

For example, an NSR value such as $0.002$ may produce a much clearer image than direct inverse filtering, although some graininess and ringing may remain.

---

## 7. Summary

Deconvolution attempts to recover an image that has been blurred by convolution.

The key ideas are:

* motion blur can be modeled as convolution with a point spread function
* the captured image is:
  $$
  g=f*h
  $$
* inverse filtering estimates the original spectrum using:
  $$
  F'=G/H
  $$
* noise becomes strongly amplified when $H$ is small
* some frequencies may be impossible to recover when $H=0$
* Wiener deconvolution suppresses unreliable frequencies
* the noise-to-signal ratio controls the balance between deblurring and noise reduction
* a constant NSR value is often used when the true frequency-dependent ratio is unknown

Deconvolution is therefore not simply the reverse of convolution. It is an estimation problem in which the blur model, missing frequencies, and noise must all be considered.
